Free annotated editions of the classics

Classic books are free to read, but full of words, places, and references that modern readers don’t know. Open Margins sets a short note in the margin wherever you are likely to stop, so you can keep reading instead of searching.

Why Open Margins

A lot of people aren’t reading classic literature these days, especially in high school and college. Yet reading trains us to think, to be articulate, and to understand other people better. And these works have faced intense selection pressure: only the works of the highest literary merit are still read today. Naturally, we can conclude that there must be something in these books that makes them worth reading.

Students now often turn to SparkNotes or LitCharts, which summarize the texts taught in schools. I think this is worsening people’s ability to read and think well. Part of the reason people turn to these tools is that reading the text by itself is difficult, so they take the easy way out.

Open Margins is meant to fix that: to give readers a smoother, more interactive experience with the classic texts themselves.

How the notes work

There are four kinds.Notes come in four layers, each with its own printer’s mark in the margin: * vocabulary, † allusions, § history, ¶ places. You can also turn any layer off.

Beside the text.Hover over an underlined phrase and its note appears in the margin beside it; click to keep it there with its source. Move the pointer into either margin to see every note on that side. On a phone, tap a phrase and the note opens below the paragraph.

Drafted by AI, checked by humans.Notes are drafted with AI, then checked by a person against the source each one cites.

Open.Every note is released under CC BY 4.0. Teachers, students, and other projects may copy and reuse them with credit.

Help check the notes

You can be a part of this project.

Of course, to scale, we can't go through every note before publishing the annoations, but we think that it is better to release something imperfect than wait until its perfect. But you can help. Pick a chapter, check each note against a reliable source, then fix it, cut it, or mark it verified and open a pull request on GitHub.

Here the margins are open: anyone can read the notes, reuse them, or help correct them.
